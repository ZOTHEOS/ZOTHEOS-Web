Present-day country

Brazil

26 nations list Brazil among their present-day countries.

Present-day countries are listed by ISO 3166 names. Many nations live across more than one.

26 nations

  1. Ashaninka

    Also known as Ashenĩka

    Lands Upper Juruá region of Acre, Brazil, including the Amônia, Breu and Envira rivers, near the Peruvian border

    Ashaninka of the Amônia river created their own association, Apiwtxa, in 1991; it was officially registered in 1993.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  2. A'uwe

    Also known as Xavante

    Lands Eastern Mato Grosso, Brazil, in Indigenous lands such as Pimentel Barbosa, Marãiwatsédé, São Marcos and Sangradouro

    The Xavante numbered about 22,256 people in 2020.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  3. Avá Guarani

    Also known as Ñandeva

    Lands Mato Grosso do Sul and Paraná in Brazil, and eastern Paraguay

    Guarani peoples, the Avá Guarani among them, live across a territory that spans regions of Brazil, Bolivia, Paraguay and Argentina.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  4. Baniwa

    Also known as Walimanai

    Lands Içana river and the Upper Rio Negro in Amazonas, Brazil, and neighboring Colombia and Venezuela

    The Baniwa speak languages of the Arawak family along the Içana river.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  5. Guarani Kaiowá

    Also known as Kaiowá

    Lands Southern Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil, and neighboring eastern Paraguay, where the people are known as Paĩ Tavyterã

    Guarani communities today call the places they live tekoha, a word that joins land, forest, fields, water, animals, plants and medicines.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  6. Guarani Mbya

    Also known as Mbyá

    Lands Villages across southern and southeastern Brazil, eastern Paraguay, Misiones province in Argentina, and near Montevideo in Uruguay

    The Comissão Guarani Yvyrupa, founded in 2006, brings together Guarani collectives in six states of southern and southeastern Brazil.

    Source: Comissão Guarani Yvyrupa (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  7. Huni Kuin

    Lands Rivers of Acre state in western Brazil, such as the Tarauacá, Jordão and Purus, and the Purus and Curanja rivers in Peru

    MAHKU, the Huni Kuin Artists Movement founded in Acre in 2013, painted a large mural across the facade of the Central Pavilion at the 2024 Venice Biennale.

    Source: La Biennale di Venezia (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  8. Ka'apor

    Lands Northern Maranhão, Brazil, in the Alto Turiaçu Indigenous Land

    Ka'apor communities have had a shared, standard sign language for communicating with deaf members of the community.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  9. Kaliña

    Also known as Kari'na

    Lands Coastal Suriname and French Guiana, with related communities in Guyana, Venezuela and Brazil

    In Suriname the Kari'na (Kaliña) live in many villages with their own village heads, including Christiaankondre and Langamankondre at Galibi in Marowijne district.

    Source: Vereniging van Inheemse Dorpshoofden in Suriname (VIDS) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  10. Kuikuro

    Lands Upper Xingu, in the Xingu Indigenous Territory, Mato Grosso, Brazil

    Kuikuro is a southern Carib language.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  11. Macuxi

    Also known as Makuxi

    Lands Fields and mountains of northern Roraima, Brazil, between the headwaters of the Branco and Rupununi rivers, extending into Guyana and Venezuela

    The Conselho Indígena de Roraima, founded in 1990, unites ten peoples including the Macuxi and Wapichana, with 270 of the state's 465 communities as members.

    Source: Conselho Indígena de Roraima (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  12. Mẽbêngôkre

    Also known as Kayapó

    Lands Forests and savannas of southern Pará and northern Mato Grosso, Brazil, along rivers of the Xingu basin such as the Iriri, Bacajá and Fresco

    The Associação Floresta Protegida, created in 1998 by Mẽbêngôkre leaders, represents about 3,000 people in 31 villages across three Indigenous lands.

  13. Munduruku

    Also known as Wuy jugu

    Lands Tapajós river basin in Pará, with communities in Amazonas and Mato Grosso, Brazil

    In September 2024 Brazil's Justice Minister signed the declaration of the Sawré Muybu Indigenous Land, 178,173 hectares along the Tapajós river in Pará.

  14. Parikwene

    Also known as Palikur

    Lands The Urukauá River in the Uaçá basin of Amapá, Brazil, and Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock, Cayenne and the lower Oyapock in French Guiana

    Parikwene families have villages in both Brazil and French Guiana and travel across the border often to visit kin and to trade.

  15. Pataxó

    Lands Villages in the far south of Bahia and the north of Minas Gerais, Brazil

    A group of Pataxó researchers has been studying their language since 1998.

  16. Pemón

    Lands Savannas of southeastern Venezuela, the Kamarang River area of Guyana, and Roraima state in Brazil

    Lands Southern Suriname along the Sipaliwini, Tapanahony and Paloemeu rivers, and the Tumucumaque Indigenous Park in Pará, Brazil

    Lands Mato Grosso do Sul, with communities in Mato Grosso and São Paulo, Brazil

    Also known as Magüta

    Lands Along the upper Solimões (Amazon) river in Amazonas, Brazil, and in neighboring Peru and Colombia

  20. Tupinambá

    Also known as Tupinambá de Olivença

    Lands Atlantic Forest of southern Bahia around Olivença, in Ilhéus, Buerarema and Una, with Tupinambá communities also in Pará, Brazil

    Lands Wajãpi Indigenous Land in Amapá, Brazil, and the middle and upper Oyapock River in French Guiana

  23. Wapichana

    Also known as Wapichan

    Lands Northeastern Roraima, Brazil, and the South Rupununi in southwestern Guyana

  24. Wayana

    Lands Upper Maroni River and its tributaries in French Guiana, the Tapanahony and Paloemeu rivers in Suriname, and the East Paru River in Pará, Brazil

  25. Yanomami

    Also known as Ianomâmi

    Lands Forests of Amazonas and Roraima states in northern Brazil, continuing across the border into southern Venezuela

    About 31,000 people live in the Yanomami Indigenous Territory in Brazil, in roughly 384 villages of the Yanomami and Ye'kwana peoples.

Website privacy

On this device: these pages save your theme and motion choice in browser storage, and the nations page keeps a random key and its place in the walk there, so you meet new nations. None of it leaves your device.

Connections: Nations pages load their resources from zotheos.org. Sources and nations' own sites open when you follow a link. The separate Theater automatically loads publisher thumbnails and the selected ready player on opening the page. Their providers receive your IP address and request information under their own privacy policies. Eligible previews may start muted when in view; other stories wait for Play. A correction is written by your own mail client to contact@zotheos.org.

Roots and gratitude

ZOTHEOS draws its visual language from ancient Mesoamerica: the stepped fret, the fine black line of the painted codices, the count of days. It honors the Indigenous peoples of the Americas: the Nahua, Maya, Zapotec and many other peoples of Mexico and Central America, and the Native nations further north. They are distinct peoples with connected roots that are still living, and many Mexican and Mexican American families carry those roots too. As the project grows, it intends to work with Indigenous scholars and community members, so that every symbol here is used accurately and with permission. Any wider work with the traditions of other peoples is meant to follow the same rule: their guidance, their permission. That work has a first page: Indigenous nations today, their own names first, every fact with its source and the date it was checked.
