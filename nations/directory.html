United States inclusion

Every nation belongs. Coverage is still growing.

Federal recognition is not a definition of Indigenous identity. The federal name list is one source. Separate state directories and nations outside recognition need their own records, sources and identity review.

State-level source inventories preserved here: Alabama, New Jersey, North Carolina, Virginia. 46 other state source-discovery scopes remain pending. This measures research work on state directories, not how many nations exist or a percentage of US coverage.

  • Communities outside recognition lists. Use their own names and public sources, including independent and self-identifying communities. Do not require federal or state recognition for inclusion.
  • Native Hawaiian peoples and communities. Use Native Hawaiian-led sources. Peoples, communities and representative organizations require distinct records. A mainland tribal recognition list cannot define this coverage.
  • Alaska villages, peoples and organizations. Keep Tribal governments, broader peoples, village communities and Native corporations distinct. The federal Alaska section does not replace these separate identity reviews.
  • Cross-border and wider US-associated communities. Research nations across borders, urban communities, the District of Columbia and US-associated territories with their own sources. Geography does not confine identity or establish land rights.

State and country labels organize research. They do not draw homelands, establish ownership or confine a people to one jurisdiction. Names appearing in several inventories remain separate source records until an explicit identity review links them.
