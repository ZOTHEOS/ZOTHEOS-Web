Two Peoples And Forced Relocation

The Tribe's history identifies Otoe and Missouria as related but distinct peoples with a shared later history. It describes centuries of trade along the Missouri River, population losses from disease and warfare, and Missouria survivors living with Otoe relatives in the late eighteenth century. The account dates confinement on Nebraska's Big Blue River reservation to 1855 and removal to Red Rock, Oklahoma, to 1881. It also records broken promises, pressure to abandon established livelihoods and boarding-school punishment for speaking the language. Those accounts explain dispossession without reducing living people to loss; historical dates do not define the beginning of either people.

Preservation And Language Led By The Tribe

The Tribe's preservation page records an application in spring 2015 and National Park Service recognition of its preservation office in spring 2016. It describes review of federal projects and consultation concerning places important to the Tribe; that process is not a guarantee that a project will be stopped. A separately dated May 2026 story reports an April language-learning banquet and learning through youth programs, a local school and online classes. These are practical, community-led forms of continuity. Office recognition is not Tribal recognition, and the reported event does not certify today's course availability or language outcomes.

Roots and gratitude

ZOTHEOS draws its visual language from ancient Mesoamerica: the stepped fret, the fine black line of the painted codices, the count of days. It honors the Indigenous peoples of the Americas: the Nahua, Maya, Zapotec and many other peoples of Mexico and Central America, and the Native nations further north. They are distinct peoples with connected roots that are still living, and many Mexican and Mexican American families carry those roots too. As the project grows, it intends to work with Indigenous scholars and community members, so that every symbol here is used accurately and with permission. Any wider work with the traditions of other peoples is meant to follow the same rule: their guidance, their permission. That work has a first page: Indigenous nations today, their own names first, every fact with its source and the date it was checked.
