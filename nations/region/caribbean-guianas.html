The Caribbean, Trinidad and the Guianas

16 nations of The Caribbean, Trinidad and the Guianas, each with its own name first and checked facts with sources.

16 nations

  1. Akawaio

    Also known as Kapon

    Lands Upper Mazaruni area and nearby river valleys of western Guyana, with Akawaio also counted in Venezuela's census

    Akawaio is one of the nine Indigenous languages covered by Guyana's language revival programme, which the government moved to restart in 2021.

    Source: Department of Public Information, Government of Guyana (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  2. Barí

    Also known as Motilón Barí

    Lands Serranía de los Motilones and the Catatumbo and Oro river basins in Norte de Santander, Colombia, and Zulia state, Venezuela

    Census counts put the Barí at about 2,841 people in Venezuela in 2011 and 3,018 in Colombia in 2018.

    Source: Minority Rights Group (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  3. Parikwene

    Also known as Palikur

    Lands The Urukauá River in the Uaçá basin of Amapá, Brazil, and Saint-Georges-de-l'Oyapock, Cayenne and the lower Oyapock in French Guiana

    Parikwene families have villages in both Brazil and French Guiana and travel across the border often to visit kin and to trade.

    Source: Instituto Socioambiental (ISA), Indigenous Peoples in Brazil (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  4. Patamona

    Also known as Kapon

    Lands North and South Pakaraima Mountains and the Essequibo region of Guyana, including villages near Kaieteur Falls

    Chenapou is the Patamona village closest to Kaieteur National Park, and Patamona communities hunt, fish and gather plants in the lands around Kaieteur.

    Source: Cultural Survival (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  5. Pemón

    Also known as Arekuna

    Lands Savannas of southeastern Venezuela, the Kamarang River area of Guyana, and Roraima state in Brazil

    In Venezuela's 2011 census, where people self-identified, the Pemón made up about 4.2 percent of the country's Indigenous population.

    Source: Instituto Nacional de Estadística (INE), Venezuela (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  6. Santa Rosa First Peoples Community

    Also known as Santa Rosa Carib Community

    Lands Arima, Trinidad, Trinidad and Tobago

    The community works to pass its culture on to younger generations and the wider public, serving as cultural ambassadors for Trinidad's First Peoples.

    Source: National Trust of Trinidad and Tobago (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  7. Taíno

    Lands Puerto Rico, Cuba, the Dominican Republic and other islands of the Greater Antilles, and diaspora communities in the United States

    Over about four decades a Taíno movement has grown in Cuba, the Dominican Republic and Puerto Rico, as more families and groups affirm their Native ancestry.

    Source: Smithsonian Institution (National Museum of the American Indian and Smithsonian Latino Center) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  8. Tarëno

    Also known as Trio

    Lands Southern Suriname along the Sipaliwini, Tapanahony and Paloemeu rivers, and the Tumucumaque Indigenous Park in Pará, Brazil

    Suriname's association of Indigenous village leaders counts the Trio among the country's four large Indigenous peoples, living mainly in the south.

    Source: Vereniging van Inheemse Dorpshoofden in Suriname (VIDS) (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  9. Teko

    Also known as Émerillon

    Lands Camopi on the Oyapock River and Cayodé on the upper Maroni, French Guiana

    France lists Teko, formerly called Émerillon, among French Guiana's six Amerindian languages recognized as languages of France.

    Source: Ministère de la Culture, Direction des affaires culturelles de Guyane (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  10. Uwottüja

    Also known as Piaroa

    Lands The Autana, Cuao, Sipapo and Guayapo rivers and the middle Orinoco, Amazonas state, Venezuela

    In 2020 Uwottüja communities of the Autana, Cuao, Sipapo and Guayapo rivers met at Pendare and declared themselves guardians of their territory.

    Source: Pueblo Indígena Uwottüja, published by the Observatorio de Ecología Política de Venezuela (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  11. Wai Wai

    Also known as Waiwai

    Lands Kanashen in the far south of Guyana, and Indigenous lands in Pará, Amazonas and Roraima states, Brazil

    In 2007 the Wai Wai of Kanashen in southern Guyana made their titled forest a Community Owned Conservation Area; in 2017 it became a national protected area.

    Source: Protected Areas Commission, Guyana (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  12. Wajãpi

    Also known as Wayãpi

    Lands Wajãpi Indigenous Land in Amapá, Brazil, and the middle and upper Oyapock River in French Guiana

    UNESCO lists the oral and graphic expressions of the Wajãpi, including the kusiwa graphic art, on its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

    Source: UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  13. Warao

    Also known as Warrau

    Lands Orinoco Delta of Venezuela, the North West District of Guyana, and communities in Trinidad and Tobago

    Trinidad and Tobago's government names the Warao Nation of Trinidad and Tobago among the country's own Indigenous groups.

    Source: Ministry of Foreign and CARICOM Affairs, Government of the Republic of Trinidad and Tobago (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  14. Wayana

    Lands Upper Maroni River and its tributaries in French Guiana, the Tapanahony and Paloemeu rivers in Suriname, and the East Paru River in Pará, Brazil

    France recognizes Wayana as one of six Amerindian languages of French Guiana with the status of a language of France.

    Source: Ministère de la Culture, Direction des affaires culturelles de Guyane (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  15. Ye'kwana

    Also known as Ye'kuana

    Lands Amazonas and Bolívar states in southern Venezuela, and the Auaris and Uraricoera rivers of Roraima, Brazil

    The name Ye'kwana, which the people use for themselves, entered Venezuela's census in 1982 and has almost fully replaced the outside name Makiritare.

    Source: Instituto Nacional de Estadística (INE), Venezuela (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

  16. Yukpa

    Lands Sierra de Perijá on both sides of the border: Zulia state, Venezuela, and Cesar department, Colombia

    Census counts put the Yukpa at about 10,640 people in Venezuela in 2011 and 3,610 in Colombia in 2018.

    Source: Minority Rights Group (opens in a new tab) · Checked 8 Oct 2026

    Open this nation

Website privacy

On this device: these pages save your theme and motion choice in browser storage, and the nations page keeps a random key and its place in the walk there, so you meet new nations. None of it leaves your device.

Connections: every request these pages make goes to zotheos.org. Sources and nations' own sites open only when you follow a link. A correction is written by your own mail client to contact@zotheos.org.

Roots and gratitude

ZOTHEOS draws its visual language from ancient Mesoamerica: the stepped fret, the fine black line of the painted codices, the count of days. It honors the Indigenous peoples of the Americas: the Nahua, Maya, Zapotec and many other peoples of Mexico and Central America, and the Native nations further north. They are distinct peoples with connected roots that are still living, and many Mexican and Mexican American families carry those roots too. As the project grows, it intends to work with Indigenous scholars and community members, so that every symbol here is used accurately and with permission. Any wider work with the traditions of other peoples is meant to follow the same rule: their guidance, their permission. That work has a first page: Indigenous nations today, their own names first, every fact with its source and the date it was checked.
